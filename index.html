Frequently asked questions

What is Living With Arthritis?

Living With Arthritis is an independent registered charity in England and Wales (no. 1218461), publishing free educational information and practical resources about arthritis, exercise, everyday self-management and UK benefits. Our information does not replace personal medical advice. We are separate from Arthritis UK and the US Arthritis Foundation.

What types of arthritis do you cover?

Our resources cover conditions including osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, gout, ankylosing spondylitis and juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Related resources also discuss joint pain and fibromyalgia. Coverage is educational, not a diagnosis or a promise of specialist care for every condition.

Is arthritis an inevitable part of ageing?

No. Age can increase the risk of some types of arthritis, but age alone is not a diagnosis. Arthritis can also affect younger adults and children. Ask a GP about unexplained joint pain, swelling or stiffness that does not settle, rather than assuming it is simply ageing.

Can arthritis be cured?

There is currently no cure for most long-term forms of arthritis, but treatments can help manage symptoms and protect function. The right approach depends on the type of arthritis and your circumstances. Seek assessment and discuss treatment with your healthcare team; this website does not promise a cure.

What are the most common symptoms of arthritis?

Common symptoms include joint pain, swelling, stiffness and difficulty moving a joint. Symptoms and their pattern vary by condition. Sudden severe pain and swelling in one joint, a hot or red joint, or joint pain with fever or shivering need urgent clinical advice. Do not use a symptom list to diagnose yourself.

Does exercise make arthritis worse?

Appropriate exercise is an important part of arthritis management. For osteoarthritis, NICE recommends exercise tailored to your needs and explains that pain may increase when you first start. Regular exercise can improve pain and function over time. Ask a suitable healthcare professional how to adapt activity to your symptoms and other health conditions.

What exercises are best for arthritis?

There is no single best routine for everyone. For osteoarthritis, NICE recommends exercise tailored to the person, such as muscle strengthening and aerobic fitness. Low-impact activities can include walking, swimming or cycling when suitable. Choose and adapt activities with advice where needed, rather than copying another person's programme.

Can losing weight help arthritis pain?

For people with osteoarthritis who are living with overweight or obesity, NICE says weight loss can reduce pain and improve physical function and quality of life. The goal and support should suit the individual. This does not mean everyone with arthritis needs to lose weight or that weight loss replaces other treatment.

Is arthritis only a joint condition?

Not always. Some inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, can affect other parts of the body, such as the eyes, lungs, heart or blood vessels. Effects differ between conditions and people. Tell your healthcare team about new symptoms rather than assuming they are an ordinary flare.

How can physiotherapy help arthritis?

Physiotherapy can help improve strength and mobility and support a suitable exercise and self-management plan. The assessment and advice should reflect your condition and goals. Reading an exercise guide on this website is not the same as receiving an individual physiotherapy assessment or treatment.

Can young people get arthritis?

Yes. Arthritis can affect children, teenagers and younger adults as well as older people. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis affects children under 16. Persistent unexplained joint pain, swelling or stiffness should be assessed by an appropriate healthcare professional.

What is self-management?

Self-management means the everyday actions you take alongside your healthcare team to manage a long-term condition. This can include suitable activity, taking medicines as prescribed, monitoring symptoms, adapting tasks and seeking help when needed. It does not mean managing alone or replacing necessary treatment.

How does Living With Arthritis support people?

You can read educational guides and use practical resources such as joint-exercise information, a symptom and flare tracker and UK benefits guidance. Check each page for its purpose, limitations and review status. These web resources do not provide a diagnosis or replace personal clinical care. For urgent help, use the appropriate health service.

Is the information provided by Living With Arthritis medically reviewed?

Check the stated author, sources, date and review status on each guide. Some pages disclose an editorial source check or an author's self-review; those are not independent specialist clinical review. Do not assume that every page has been independently reviewed. The information does not replace advice from your own healthcare professional.

How can I support Living With Arthritis?

You can share a useful resource or use the website's current donation and contact routes. Contact the charity to ask which volunteering or collaboration opportunities are available before making plans. Sharing information should respect other people's privacy and the rules of the place where you share it.

Free exercise programmes

Regular exercise can help people manage arthritis symptoms. NICE recommends tailored therapeutic exercise for osteoarthritis, such as strengthening and aerobic exercise; the NHS suggests low-impact activities such as walking and swimming. Our Exercise Hub brings together joint-specific routines and a printable plan generator. Choose activity suited to your needs. See NICE osteoarthritis recommendations and NHS arthritis advice.

Tai chi and gentle movement

Programmes are split by joint — knee, hip, shoulder, hand, back and ankle — and by modality including seated tai chi, gentle yoga, resistance bands and aquatic exercise. Each routine is designed to be completed at home in 10 to 20 minutes, with clear progression for beginners through to confident movers.

Anti-inflammatory diet and Mediterranean eating

A healthy, balanced diet supports general health and a healthy weight. No single food or diet cures arthritis. Our Diet Hub offers practical meal ideas, shopping lists and seven-day plans, including Mediterranean-style eating. Use these alongside your treatment plan, not instead of prescribed medicines. See NHS advice on living with arthritis.

Supplements and nutrition guidance

You will also find guidance on omega-3, turmeric and ginger, sensible weight management for knee and hip osteoarthritis, and honest reviews of supplements such as glucosamine and collagen.
