# Vitamin D Deficiency and Children With Autism: What Parents Need to Know

## Introduction

Vitamin D helps children develop strong bones and maintain normal muscle function. Research has explored whether autistic children are more likely to have low vitamin D levels. Some studies report lower average levels, but this does **not** prove that deficiency causes autism or that supplements treat autism.

## Why vitamin D matters

Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium and phosphorus. Prolonged severe deficiency can cause rickets in children. Sources include sunlight, oily fish, eggs, fortified foods and supplements. In the UK, sunlight is not usually strong enough to produce sufficient vitamin D during autumn and winter. [1]

## What the research shows

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis covering 34 studies reported an association between autism and lower vitamin D concentrations. Observational studies cannot establish cause and effect, and study methods varied. [3]

A 2023 review of eight randomised controlled trials involving 266 children found inconsistent effects of vitamin D supplementation on autism-related outcomes. Vitamin D should not be promoted as an autism treatment. [4]

## Why some children may be at risk

Some autistic children have sensory-based food preferences or restricted diets that limit certain nutrients. Less time outdoors and some medical conditions can also contribute to low vitamin D. Every child is different; autism itself does not establish a deficiency.

## Symptoms and assessment

Vitamin D deficiency may have no obvious symptoms. More severe deficiency can contribute to bone pain, muscle weakness and rickets. These symptoms have other possible causes. A GP can assess dietary intake, symptoms and risk factors and decide whether testing is needed. Not every autistic child needs a routine vitamin D blood test.

## UK supplementation guidance

NHS guidance recommends 8.5–10 micrograms daily for breastfed babies under one year from birth. Formula-fed babies generally do not need additional supplements if taking more than 500 ml of formula daily. Children aged one to four should receive 10 micrograms daily. People aged five and above should consider 10 micrograms daily during autumn and winter, with year-round supplementation for some higher-risk groups. These are prevention doses, not treatment doses for diagnosed deficiency. [1, 2]

## Practical support for families

Respect the child's sensory preferences, offer accepted nutritious foods, and introduce unfamiliar foods without pressure. A paediatric dietitian can help with a highly restricted diet. Ask a GP or pharmacist about appropriate supplements. Avoid combining multiple vitamin D products without checking the total dose, because excessive intake can be harmful.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does vitamin D deficiency cause autism?** No causal relationship has been established.

**Can vitamin D cure autism?** No. Supplements are not an autism cure or a proven general treatment for autism-related characteristics.

**Should every autistic child be tested?** No. Testing depends on individual symptoms and risk factors.

**Can excess vitamin D be harmful?** Yes. Excess intake can cause high calcium levels and other complications.

## Conclusion

Some studies find lower vitamin D levels in autistic children, but the explanation remains uncertain. Supporting adequate nutrition and appropriate supplementation is valuable for every child's health, regardless of autism diagnosis.
