## Article 1: What Causes Gout? Understanding Uric Acid, Triggers and Prevention

**Primary keyword:** what causes gout

### What causes gout?
Gout is a type of inflammatory arthritis caused by deposits of monosodium urate crystals in and around joints. These crystals form when the level of uric acid, also called urate, in the blood remains high enough for crystals to develop. However, not everyone with a raised urate level develops gout.

Uric acid is produced when the body breaks down substances called purines, which occur naturally in cells and in some foods. The kidneys normally remove much of this uric acid through urine. In many people with gout, the main problem is that the kidneys do not remove enough urate, rather than eating too much of a particular food.

### Who is more likely to develop gout?
Factors associated with gout include family history, increasing age, kidney disease, excess body weight and certain medicines, including some diuretics. Alcohol, particularly beer and spirits, and frequent consumption of sugary drinks can contribute. Foods high in purines, such as offal and some seafood, may also increase risk, but diet is only one part of the picture.

### What triggers a gout attack?
An attack may begin suddenly, often overnight, with severe pain, warmth, swelling and redness in a joint. The big toe is commonly affected, but gout can also involve the ankle, knee, wrist or fingers. Dehydration, illness, surgery and rapid changes in urate levels can sometimes trigger attacks.

### Can gout be treated?
Yes. During an attack, a clinician may recommend an anti-inflammatory medicine, colchicine or a corticosteroid, depending on your medical history. For people with recurrent gout or other indications, urate-lowering treatment such as allopurinol can reduce future attacks and complications. Treatment is usually monitored with blood tests and adjusted to a target urate level.

### Living with gout
Drink adequate fluids unless advised otherwise, discuss medicines with your clinician and aim for sustainable lifestyle changes. Do not stop prescribed medication without advice. With appropriate long-term treatment, many people can prevent attacks and protect their joints.

**Important:** A suddenly hot, swollen joint accompanied by fever or feeling unwell needs urgent medical assessment because joint infection can resemble gout.

## Article 2: Shoulder Pain: Common Causes, Symptoms and When to Seek Help

**Primary keyword:** shoulder pain  
**Secondary keywords:** painful shoulder, pain in the shoulder, pain on shoulder, pain shoulder  
**Suggested URL:** `/blog/shoulder-pain-causes-treatment`  
**Meta description:** Learn why shoulder pain happens, how to manage a painful shoulder safely and which warning signs need urgent medical attention.

### Why does shoulder pain happen?
Shoulder pain is common and can affect reaching, dressing, sleeping and everyday activities. The shoulder relies on several joints, tendons and muscles working together. Pain may develop after an injury, with repetitive activity or gradually without a clear cause.

Common causes include rotator cuff-related shoulder pain, frozen shoulder, osteoarthritis and pain referred from the neck. Bursitis and tendon problems can also contribute. The location of pain alone does not reliably identify the diagnosis, so an assessment may be needed if symptoms persist.

### How do different shoulder problems feel?
Rotator cuff-related pain often becomes worse when lifting the arm or reaching overhead. Frozen shoulder typically causes progressive pain and marked stiffness, including difficulty moving the shoulder even when someone else tries to help. Osteoarthritis may produce stiffness, discomfort and reduced movement over time. Neck-related pain may travel into the arm and be accompanied by tingling or numbness.

### What can help a painful shoulder?
For many non-urgent shoulder problems, gentle movement and gradual return to activity are more helpful than prolonged rest. Try comfortable shoulder movements throughout the day and modify tasks that repeatedly aggravate symptoms. Avoid forcing painful stretches or starting heavy exercises suddenly.

A physiotherapist can assess movement, strength and functional difficulties, then recommend an individualised programme. Depending on the cause, treatment may include exercise, pain relief, advice on sleep positioning or further assessment. Anti-inflammatory medicines are not suitable for everyone, particularly people with certain kidney, stomach or cardiovascular conditions.

### When should you get medical help?
Seek prompt assessment if pain follows a fall or injury, you cannot lift your arm, you develop significant weakness, or the shoulder is red, hot or swollen. Chest pain, breathlessness, sweating or pain spreading to the jaw or arm can indicate a medical emergency: call 999.

If pain is persistent, interrupts sleep or affects work and daily living, speak with your GP or an appropriate musculoskeletal clinician. A clear diagnosis helps avoid unnecessary treatments and supports a safe recovery.

## Article 3: Turmeric for Pain Relief: Does It Help Arthritis and Inflammation?

**Primary keyword:** turmeric for pain relief  
**Secondary keywords:** turmeric and pain, turmeric for pain, turmeric and pain relief, turmeric for inflammation, does turmeric help with inflammation, turmeric as an anti inflammatory

**Meta description:** Can turmeric reduce arthritis pain or inflammation? Explore the evidence, potential side effects and important medication interactions.

### What is turmeric?
Turmeric is a spice derived from the Curcuma longa plant. It contains curcuminoids, including curcumin, which have been studied for anti-inflammatory effects. Turmeric is widely used in cooking, while concentrated curcumin extracts are sold as supplements.

### Does turmeric help with pain?
Some small clinical trials and reviews suggest that certain curcumin preparations may modestly improve pain and function in people with knee osteoarthritis. However, studies vary considerably in dose, formulation and quality. The evidence is not strong enough to conclude that turmeric reliably treats arthritis, and findings from one supplement cannot automatically be applied to another.

There is also insufficient high-quality evidence to recommend turmeric as a treatment for rheumatoid arthritis or fibromyalgia. It should not replace prescribed disease-modifying treatment, physiotherapy or other evidence-based care.

### Is turmeric anti-inflammatory?
Laboratory research suggests curcumin can influence inflammatory pathways. That does not necessarily mean taking turmeric will produce the same effects in the human body. Curcumin is poorly absorbed in many formulations, and products designed to increase absorption can also alter interactions and safety.

### Are there risks?
Using turmeric in ordinary food amounts is generally different from taking high-dose supplements. Concentrated products can cause stomach upset and, rarely, liver injury. Some formulations contain piperine, an ingredient from black pepper that can affect the handling of medicines. Turmeric supplements may be unsuitable for people taking anticoagulants or other interacting medicines, and for some people with gallbladder or liver problems.

Speak to a pharmacist or clinician before starting a supplement, especially if you are pregnant, have a long-term condition or take regular medication. Do not assume a product labelled “natural” is risk-free.

### What works alongside dietary choices?
For arthritis pain, a balanced approach may include appropriate exercise, sleep support, weight management where relevant and medicines matched to the diagnosis. For inflammatory arthritis, early specialist assessment and disease-modifying treatment can prevent joint damage.

**Bottom line:** Turmeric may have limited benefits for some people with osteoarthritis, but it is not a proven substitute for standard treatment.

## Article 4: Signs of Arthritis: Early Symptoms and When to See a GP

**Primary keyword:** signs of arthritis

**Meta description:** Recognise common early signs of arthritis, including joint pain, swelling and stiffness, and learn when to seek medical advice.

### What are the signs of arthritis?
Arthritis is a broad term describing conditions that affect joints, and symptoms depend on the underlying diagnosis. Common signs include joint pain, stiffness, swelling, tenderness and difficulty with movement. Some people notice reduced grip strength, trouble climbing stairs or discomfort after everyday activities.

Osteoarthritis and inflammatory arthritis can share symptoms, but they are not the same condition and may need very different treatment.

### Early signs of osteoarthritis
Osteoarthritis commonly affects the knees, hips, hands and spine. Symptoms often develop gradually, with pain related to activity and stiffness after resting. Morning stiffness is typically relatively brief, although experiences vary. Affected joints may feel less flexible or make grinding sensations during movement.

### Early signs of inflammatory arthritis
Inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis may cause persistent joint swelling, warmth and prolonged morning stiffness. Symptoms can affect several joints, including the small joints of the hands and feet, and may occur alongside fatigue. Psoriatic arthritis can cause swollen fingers or toes, tendon insertion pain and changes in the nails.

Not every person experiences the textbook pattern. Early inflammatory arthritis may be present even if initial blood tests are normal.

### How is arthritis diagnosed?
A clinician will ask about the pattern and duration of symptoms and examine affected joints. Depending on the suspected condition, blood tests, X-rays, ultrasound or other investigations may be useful. No single blood test diagnoses every form of arthritis.

If persistent swelling or inflammatory symptoms suggest rheumatoid arthritis, timely referral is important because early treatment can reduce the risk of permanent joint damage.

### When should you seek help?
Contact your GP if joint symptoms last, recur or interfere with daily activities. A suddenly painful, hot and swollen joint requires urgent assessment, particularly with fever or feeling unwell, because infection must be ruled out.

### Supporting your joints
While waiting for assessment, stay gently active within your abilities and avoid repeatedly pushing through severe pain. A physiotherapist can advise on suitable exercises, and a clinician can help choose safe pain relief.

## Article 5: Can I Claim PIP for Arthritis? A UK Guide to Eligibility

**Primary keyword:** can i claim pip for arthritis  
**Secondary keywords:** claiming pip for arthritis, can i claim pip for osteoarthritis, can i get pip for osteoarthritis, how to claim pip successfully for arthritis

### Can you claim PIP if you have arthritis?
Arthritis can affect eligibility for Personal Independence Payment (PIP), but having a diagnosis does not automatically qualify someone for the benefit. Decisions focus on how a long-term health condition affects specific daily living and mobility activities, not simply on the condition's name.

PIP rules apply mainly to eligible working-age claimants in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Scotland uses Adult Disability Payment for new applications instead. Age, residence and other eligibility rules apply, and benefit arrangements can change.

### What difficulties are considered?
Daily living activities include preparing food, washing, dressing, managing treatment and communicating. Mobility activities include planning and following journeys and moving around. Arthritis may make some of these tasks painful, slow, unsafe or difficult to complete repeatedly.

Assessments consider whether activities can be carried out reliably: safely, to an acceptable standard, repeatedly and within a reasonable time. The way symptoms vary over time matters, so it is important to describe both better and worse days accurately.

### What evidence can support a claim?
Useful information may include medical letters, medication lists, physiotherapy or occupational therapy reports and details of aids or adaptations. A personal diary describing specific difficulties can help explain the practical impact of symptoms.

Give concrete examples. Rather than writing only “my knees hurt,” explain whether you need to sit while preparing food, how long dressing takes or whether you can walk a particular distance safely and repeatedly. Do not exaggerate symptoms or minimise genuine difficulties.

### Can you work and receive PIP?
PIP is not means-tested and employment does not automatically prevent an award. The relevant question is how your condition affects the assessed activities. Other benefits have different rules.

### Where can you get help?
Check current official eligibility and application information on GOV.UK or nidirect, or Social Security Scotland for Adult Disability Payment. Citizens Advice and independent welfare-rights services can help with forms, evidence, mandatory reconsiderations and appeals.

**Important:** This is general information, not individual benefits or legal advice. Verify the latest rules before applying.
