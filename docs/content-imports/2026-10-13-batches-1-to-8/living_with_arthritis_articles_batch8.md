# How to Claim PIP Successfully for Arthritis: A Practical UK Guide

## What does PIP assess?
Personal Independence Payment (PIP) is a UK benefit intended to help eligible people with extra costs arising from long-term health conditions or disabilities. A diagnosis of arthritis alone does not guarantee an award. Decision-makers consider how your condition affects specific daily living and mobility activities, using prescribed descriptors and scoring rules. PIP is generally for people claiming before State Pension age; different arrangements apply in Scotland, where Adult Disability Payment has replaced new PIP claims.

## Describe what happens on difficult days
Explain exactly what you can and cannot do. Arthritis may affect washing, dressing, preparing food, managing treatment, walking or planning and following journeys. Rather than writing “my knees hurt”, describe how far you can walk, how long it takes, whether you need to stop, and what happens afterwards. Note if you need assistance, supervision or an aid. Explain fluctuations: how often symptoms worsen and what a typical week looks like.

The reliability rules are particularly important. An activity must generally be achievable safely, to an acceptable standard, repeatedly and within a reasonable time. Being able to perform something once does not necessarily mean you can do it reliably.

## Gather useful supporting evidence
Evidence might include clinic letters, medication lists, occupational therapy reports, physiotherapy assessments and a symptom diary. Ask someone who helps you regularly to describe the support they provide. Medical records can confirm diagnoses, but a specific explanation of functional limitations is often more useful than a long list of conditions.

Complete forms accurately and consistently. Give concrete examples of accidents, falls, fatigue, pain or the assistance you require. Do not exaggerate or minimise your difficulties. Keep copies of forms and supporting documents, and respond to requests by the stated deadlines.

## What if your claim is refused?
Read the decision letter carefully. You can usually ask for a mandatory reconsideration, normally within one month of the decision, and may then appeal to an independent tribunal. Seek free support from Citizens Advice or a welfare rights adviser. In Scotland, follow the separate Adult Disability Payment review and appeal process.

**Key point:** A successful claim depends on meeting the legal criteria and showing your actual functional needs, not on using particular phrases.

# Is Arthritis Classed as a Disability in the UK?

## Does arthritis automatically count as a disability?
Arthritis can be a disability, but not every person with arthritis will meet the legal definition. Under the Equality Act 2010, which applies in Great Britain, a disability generally involves a physical or mental impairment with a substantial and long-term adverse effect on normal day-to-day activities. “Substantial” means more than minor or trivial; “long-term” usually means lasting, or likely to last, at least 12 months.

The assessment is about the effect of your condition, not simply the diagnostic label. Joint pain, stiffness, fatigue, restricted movement and flare-ups may affect dressing, walking, carrying objects or working. Conditions that fluctuate can still meet the definition. Northern Ireland has separate disability discrimination legislation.

## What rights might apply at work?
If your arthritis meets the relevant legal test, an employer may have a duty to make reasonable adjustments. Examples could include ergonomic equipment, altered duties, flexible breaks, changes to working hours or a more accessible workstation. What is reasonable depends on your circumstances and the employer's situation.

You do not necessarily need to stop working because of arthritis. Occupational health, physiotherapy and workplace assessments may identify practical changes that help you remain employed. Discuss your functional needs with your employer rather than relying on a diagnosis alone.

## Is disability status the same as benefit eligibility?
No. Equality Act protection and eligibility for PIP, Adult Disability Payment or other benefits are different legal questions. PIP considers defined daily living and mobility activities. Employment and Support Allowance and Universal Credit have other rules. A person might qualify under one scheme but not another.

For tailored benefits advice, consult Citizens Advice or a qualified welfare rights adviser. For employment rights, ACAS offers guidance in Great Britain, while the Labour Relations Agency serves Northern Ireland.

## Managing daily life
Small adjustments may make daily activities easier: use larger-handled utensils, distribute loads across both hands, plan activity breaks and follow an individualised exercise programme. Speak to a GP, physiotherapist or occupational therapist if pain or fatigue affects independence.

**Remember:** The question is how arthritis affects your life over time. Keep examples of limitations and support needs, especially if symptoms vary.

# Hand Exercises for Arthritis: Gentle Movement and Strength

## Can hand exercises help arthritis?
Gentle movement and strengthening can support hand function in people with osteoarthritis or inflammatory arthritis. Exercise cannot reverse joint damage, but a suitable programme may help maintain movement, improve confidence and make everyday tasks easier. The best approach depends on which joints are affected, whether there is active inflammation and your current strength.

Start slowly and work within a comfortable range. Mild effort is expected, but sharp pain, marked swelling or symptoms that remain worse after exercise are signs to reduce the intensity and seek advice.

## Five gentle exercises to discuss with a clinician
**Finger opening:** Rest your hand on a table, palm down. Slowly spread your fingers apart, then bring them together. Repeat several times without forcing movement.

**Finger bends:** With your wrist supported, gently bend your fingers towards your palm, then straighten them. Move only as far as comfortable.

**Thumb opposition:** Touch the tip of your thumb to each fingertip in turn, making a small circle. Avoid pushing through pain at the base of the thumb.

**Tendon gliding:** Begin with straight fingers, gently form a hook shape, then a loose fist, and return to straight fingers. A hand therapist can show you the safest sequence.

**Light grip work:** If advised, gently squeeze a soft foam ball or rolled towel, hold briefly and release. Strong gripping may aggravate some painful thumb or finger joints, so it is not suitable for everyone.

## How often should you practise?
A few comfortable repetitions once or twice daily may be a reasonable starting point, but there is no single routine for every type of arthritis. Increase gradually based on your response. If joints are hot, swollen or acutely inflamed, ask your clinical team whether to modify or pause strengthening while maintaining gentle movement.

Splints, pacing, joint-protection strategies and adapted tools can also be useful. An occupational therapist or specialist hand therapist can assess individual needs.

## When should you get help?
Seek assessment for persistent swelling, prolonged morning stiffness, worsening weakness, numbness or loss of function. A suddenly red, hot, very painful joint, especially with fever, needs urgent medical attention.

**Practical tip:** Keep movements controlled and comfortable. Consistency is more useful than pushing through severe pain.

# TENS Machines and Cancer: Safety Questions Explained

## What is a TENS machine?
Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) uses small electrical pulses delivered through pads on the skin. Some people use it for short-term pain relief, including certain musculoskeletal conditions. Its benefits vary, and it does not treat the underlying cause of cancer or arthritis.

## Can someone with cancer use TENS?
A cancer diagnosis does not automatically answer whether TENS is appropriate. The type and location of cancer, treatment received, skin condition, implanted devices and reason for pain all matter. Some clinical guidance advises avoiding stimulation over areas of known or suspected malignancy. Because precautions differ between situations, people with current or previous cancer should ask their oncology or pain-management team before using TENS.

Do not place electrodes directly over a tumour or an area of unexplained swelling without specialist approval. Avoid irritated, broken or numb skin, where burns or skin injury may be harder to detect. If you have a pacemaker or another implanted electronic device, do not use TENS unless your specialist has specifically confirmed it is safe.

## Where should electrodes not go?
TENS pads should not be positioned across the chest in a way that passes current through the heart, over the front or sides of the neck, or near the eyes. Do not use TENS while driving, bathing or sleeping. People with epilepsy, pregnancy or other significant medical conditions should seek advice about suitability and electrode placement.

Follow the manufacturer's instructions and guidance from a qualified clinician. Start with a comfortable sensation rather than a painful intensity. Stop if you develop dizziness, palpitations, skin irritation or increased pain.

## What if cancer pain is worsening?
New or escalating pain needs assessment rather than simply stronger stimulation. Tell your cancer team promptly about persistent pain, new weakness, numbness or unexplained symptoms. Sudden severe back pain with leg weakness, bladder or bowel changes, or numbness around the saddle area requires emergency assessment because spinal cord or cauda equina compression may be possible.

Cancer pain can often be managed using a combination of medicines, rehabilitation, supportive care and specialist interventions. TENS, if appropriate, is only one possible component.

**Bottom line:** Get individual clearance from your treating team before trying TENS when cancer is involved.

# Medical Management of Arthritis: Treatments and Long-Term Care

## Why does the type of arthritis matter?
“Arthritis” describes several conditions, not one disease. Osteoarthritis is commonly managed through exercise, education and symptom relief. Rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis involve inflammatory pathways and may require medicines that control immune activity. Gout is caused by urate crystal deposition and has a different long-term treatment approach.

A careful diagnosis is therefore essential. A clinician may use symptoms, examination, blood tests and imaging to distinguish causes of joint pain. No single blood test confirms or excludes every form of arthritis.

## Managing osteoarthritis
Core treatments include tailored strengthening and aerobic activity, weight management where appropriate, education and support with daily tasks. Topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may help some painful joints. Oral NSAIDs may be considered when suitable, using the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary period because of gastrointestinal, kidney and cardiovascular risks.

Joint replacement may be discussed when symptoms significantly affect quality of life despite appropriate non-surgical care. Treatment should reflect the person's goals and health conditions.

## Treating inflammatory arthritis
Rheumatoid arthritis often requires prompt specialist assessment and disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), such as methotrexate, to reduce inflammation and prevent damage. Biologic or targeted synthetic therapies may be considered when disease control is inadequate. These treatments require screening and ongoing monitoring, including blood tests and infection-risk assessment.

Steroids may sometimes be used briefly for flares, but prolonged use has important risks. Do not stop prescribed DMARDs or steroids without medical advice.

## Gout and other conditions
Acute gout attacks may be treated with an NSAID, colchicine or a corticosteroid, depending on individual risks. People with recurrent or complicated gout may benefit from urate-lowering treatment, often with a target blood urate level and monitoring. The best medicine depends on kidney function, other conditions and current prescriptions.

## Building a long-term care plan
A useful plan combines medicines with physiotherapy, occupational therapy, physical activity, sleep support and regular review. Ask your clinician about treatment benefits, side effects, vaccinations and what to do during infection or a flare.

Seek urgent care for a suddenly hot, swollen joint with fever or feeling seriously unwell: joint infection must be excluded.

**Key message:** Effective arthritis care is diagnosis-specific, collaborative and regularly reviewed.
